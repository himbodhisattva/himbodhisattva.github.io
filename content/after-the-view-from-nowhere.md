# After the View from Nowhere (draft)

I was a physics major when I first encountered epistemology and Mahayana Buddhism, which is a fairly efficient way to ruin the obvious world. I learned that perception is constructed: what I experience is not simply reality arriving intact in my head. The self doing the experiencing may also be constructed. Then came the old skeptical problem underneath all of it. Try to justify a belief and you end up going in a circle, asking for another justification forever, or deciding to stop asking. Reason certifies reason by using reason. Memory certifies memory by relying on memory. How would I check perception against a reality I had never perceived?

Kant was asking what makes experience possible, and Madhyamaka Buddhism is more interesting than an elaborate proof of solipsism. I know. This is about the panic before those distinctions settle: How the hell do I know any of this is real?

For a long time I assumed Judaism had nothing useful to say here. I thought its answer had to be: Torah is true because Hashem gave it to us; we know Hashem gave it to us because Torah says so; stop asking. I could keep doubting, or I could put an arbitrary foundation under one inherited tradition and pretend the building was secure. I knew which choice a person who cared about truth was supposed to make.

This is a defense of Jewish commitment addressed to a particular reader. You studied enough Kant to become annoying about Kant. You encountered Buddhist emptiness and learned to see how identities and institutions get made. Maybe you spent twenty years becoming increasingly good at noticing the assumptions other people live inside. I am assuming you no longer need reassurance that religion can coexist with physics.

Now you are middle-aged. You have a city, a career, people who depend on you, and a normal use of Friday night. You may still feel that you haven't really chosen how to live. Meanwhile, you have been living a particular way for decades. I would have missed this argument at twenty-two and treated my failure to understand it as evidence against it.

My claim is that permanent searching can become the last eternalism. Somewhere, we still suppose, there must be a final answer that will make commitment safe. We reject every available tradition because it is incomplete or compromised, but keep expecting to find one that cannot disappoint us. We have given up on certainty everywhere except in our requirements for choosing a life. Sometimes this is fear with excellent reading comprehension, still calling itself openness.

## Who Is Doing the Doubting?

Radical skepticism delivers a real discovery: a belief cannot certify its own truth. I then made skepticism itself into a privileged position, as though exposing the failure of foundations could supply one.

David Deutsch gave me the cleanest route out. We propose explanations, criticize them, and replace worse ones with better ones. An explanation can improve on its predecessor without becoming the last word. I can be wrong about anything without being equally wrong about everything. I don't need an infallible starting point to learn.

That gets inquiry moving again. It still leaves me with questions about how to live. On Friday afternoon, I have to decide when to stop working and what to teach my children. My theory of knowledge can help me examine those decisions. It cannot postpone them until I have finished examining them.

Radical doubt seems to leave one thing standing: experience is happening. It is easy to imagine the person having that experience as a point of awareness behind the eyes, with no parents or history. Call him *homo nullius loci*, the person from nowhere. Like *homo economicus*, he is a useful abstraction that becomes misleading when mistaken for an actual person. He is the view from nowhere relocated behind the eyes.

My experience is full of things that this picture leaves out. I think in a language I didn't invent. I remember my parents. I am Jewish, married, and a father. Even the desire to stand apart and evaluate all this came from somewhere. If I start with my own experience, why would I discard most of what is in it?

My karma is mine. I am using “karma” loosely here: the past has left me with circumstances and obligations I didn't choose, and what happens next partly depends on what I do. Even the detached observer has a body, a browser history, a mortgage, and usually someone else in the house who already knows what time the children need to leave.

Buddhist emptiness can become another way to dodge this. Things arise through their relations to other things; they have no independent essence. I can understand this and then imagine that *I*, the person who understands, have escaped those relations. Madhyamaka doesn't grant that exemption. Emptiness is itself empty. My understanding of how things depend on one another also depends on things, and it doesn't place me outside the life I am trying to understand.

David Chapman calls meaning “patterned and nebulous”: it has enough shape to guide us, but it is never completely fixed. Parenthood is a good example. There is no exhaustive definition of a good father that settles every case. There is also no serious doubt that I should pick up my daughter. I can rank parenthood among contingent social roles while she waits.

Readers of James C. Scott's *Seeing Like a State* will recognize the mistake. A planner removes the local detail that makes a place work so it will fit his scheme. We do something similar when we compare religions and ways of life as though choosing from a spreadsheet. The person doing the comparing already has a history with some of those people and practices. That history may be part of what he needs to understand. Removing it doesn't automatically improve his judgment.

## Returning After Criticism

Robert Kegan's stages help explain why returning to a tradition can look like giving up. Using them loosely: at stage three, the standards of my family or community largely determine who I am and what I should do. At stage four, I can examine those standards and decide which ones I endorse. At stage five, I can examine the system I built to do the examining. My supposedly independent judgment has assumptions and blind spots too.

I move among these positions. Sometimes I can question my own standards; sometimes I am defending them; sometimes I just want someone to tell me what to do. Knowing the stage numbers doesn't settle which one I'm doing.

The temptation is to identify with the person who sees through everything. TPOT, “This Part of Twitter,” pronounced “teapot,” has often rewarded this. I love its curiosity and willingness to entertain strange possibilities. I have also used that atmosphere to remain someone who was still getting there, never quite ready for a particular claim to become binding.

Some people in TPOT became Catholic. The accounts that interest me describe beginning to practice before the practice feels natural, then discovering that it changes their judgment. [Kitten Beloved's essay about instinct and obedience](https://www.adorableandharmless.com/p/instinct-and-obedience-to-god) is one example. You try doing something that once seemed impossibly naive or inauthentic, and find that the person who dismissed it didn't know everything.

Of course, people also return to religion because they are frightened and want an authority to take over. Turning forty-five adds no sophistication to that move. From outside, it can be hard to tell the difference. Two Jews may keep the same Shabbat for different reasons; one Jew may keep it for different reasons in the course of a month.

I say Kiddush over the wine on Friday night and mean it. The objections still matter when I judge what my community does or what I am being asked to believe. Compared with the family I grew up in, I am the observant one. An observant Jew reading this would probably find my actual practice disappointing. I am still learning to carry the yoke I am describing.

This is what I mean by “stage-five honor culture.” I can understand how a standard arose and still allow it to find me lacking. If I accept an obligation only while I happen to want what it requires, it has very little authority over me. The harder thing is to let it govern me when it is inconvenient, while retaining the ability to judge that it is wrong. There is no rule that will spare me the difficulty of telling those cases apart.

Nor am I choosing from scratch. Judaism reached me before I had a theory of traditions. It helped form the person who later imagined himself choosing among them. That doesn't settle what I owe it. But I understand perfectly well that I can owe something to people I didn't choose. I don't dismiss my parents' claims on me because being born to them was an accident of history. Why should that observation alone dispose of my people's claim?

Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* is in the background here: what happens to moral judgments when they are detached from the shared practices that once gave them sense? Our subculture has assembled a lot of useful criticism from traditions we no longer inhabit. We can spot self-deception, question inherited authority, and explain why identities are unstable. We still have to work out what to do together, and what to pass on.

## Deutsch and Hanson

To test the argument, I imagined David Deutsch as the most ecstatic *baal teshuvah* in history, a Jew returning to religious observance, and Robin Hanson as an equally improbable Orthodox convert. Deutsch asks whether the tradition is true. Hanson asks what its costs and institutions do.

Deutsch hears about community and inherited meaning and objects immediately. Every cult offers those. People can find purpose in falsehood. A tradition can survive by punishing criticism and hiding evidence against its claims. Feeling at home in it is no proof of its theology. A Jewish argument has to answer this objection without asking him to stop caring whether things are true.

The rabbinic use of *lo bashamayim hi*, “it is not in heaven,” in Bava Metzia 59b gives me a more interesting starting point. A heavenly voice enters a legal dispute, and the rabbis refuse to let it settle the case. Torah has been given to human beings. They must interpret it through human procedures, even here.

The authority to decide is itself part of what Hashem has given them. In the Talmud, disputes and minority opinions survive alongside rulings that bind practice. The community can decide what to do without pretending that the losing argument never existed.

None of this guarantees good judgment. [Jews and Jewish institutions can preserve error with tremendous stamina](https://himbodhisattva.com/blog/rav-uziel-the-gates-we-guard/). The point is that disagreement has an established place inside the tradition. There are religious reasons to argue about what the law requires, and ways to do so without first resigning from Jewish life. That makes room for the Deutschian returnee. It does not answer every objection he will bring with him.

Then Hanson asks the impolite question: why Judaism? American Buddhism is cheaper. You can learn to examine experience and live with doubt without reorganizing your kitchen or giving up Saturday work. Why buy the expensive product?

I mean the consumer version many of us encountered. Buddhist traditions also have vows and demanding communal lives. The comparison concerns what we were shopping for: help feeling better about uncertainty, with few additional obligations.

Judaism is trying to carry a whole way of life across generations. It tells me that I am someone's child and someone's ancestor, hopefully many someones'. Shabbat takes time I could sell. Kashrut makes eating less convenient. Prayer asks me to show up, including with people I would not have selected by taste. Tzedakah requires giving money; sexual laws constrain desire. These practices give other people something they can count on beyond my current enthusiasm.

Hazing and fanaticism are expensive too. Cost proves very little by itself. But “asks less of me” is a strange advantage when I want something strong enough to organize my life. A religion that reaches into my kitchen and my children's week has some chance of surviving my next change of mood.

## Living With Uncertainty

Judaism has no single answer to the questions that first bothered me. Saadia Gaon discusses sense perception, reason, and reliable tradition as sources of knowledge. Rambam treats knowledge of the world as possible while sharply limiting what we can say about Hashem. Kabbalistic and Hasidic accounts question whether things are ultimately as separate and self-sufficient as they appear. I find that last thought congenial. The table is real enough to hold Shabbat candles even if its apparent separateness is not metaphysically final.

I don't need my senses to reveal everything about reality in order to use them. When a witness is mistaken or an instrument corrects what I thought I saw, I have to change my judgment. Religious responsibility also has to operate at this human scale. The rabbis say that a judge has only what his eyes see, and that Torah was given to human beings rather than ministering angels. We remain responsible for decisions we must make with limited understanding.

Several familiar Jewish teachings look different to me in this light.

*Na'aseh v'nishma*, “we will do and we will hear” (Exodus 24:7), places doing before full understanding. Some understanding comes from the practice itself. I can't require it all in advance as a condition of beginning.

*Aseh lecha rav, vehistalek min hasafek*, “make for yourself a teacher and remove yourself from doubt” (Pirkei Avot 1:16), gives a practical response to uncertainty. Find someone whose judgment you can rely on. I read this as advice about how to reach a decision, rather than a promise that the teacher cannot be wrong.

*Lo alecha hamelacha ligmor, velo atah ben chorin lehibatel mimena*: “it is not your responsibility to finish the work, but you are not free to desist from it” (Pirkei Avot 2:16). I keep coming back to this one. My inability to complete the work doesn't release me from my share of it.

Rashi's reading of *tamim tihyeh im Hashem Elokecha* asks us to walk wholeheartedly with Hashem and stop trying to know the future (Deuteronomy 18:13). I hear a rebuke to my demand to see how everything turns out before I commit.

These teachings come from people living inside a tradition, already facing the problems of disagreement and incomplete knowledge. They developed ways to reach decisions and learn from practice. I can return to those ways after questioning the tradition. The problems haven't gone away.

In this setting, *emunah*, usually translated as faith, can name fidelity and trust: keeping faith with Hashem through what I do, even while I have unanswered questions. A *baal teshuvah* is, literally enough for this argument, someone who turned.

## Now Is the Combat

The *Bhagavad Gita* supplies a comparison. Arjuna sees the horror of the battlefield and wants to withdraw. Krishna gives him metaphysical instruction and a vision of the divine form. Arjuna returns to his duty. The enlarged view brings him back to the place where he must act.

Epictetus makes a similar demand without the cosmology. “Now is the combat,” he says in section 51 of the *Enchiridion*. There is no later occasion when I will finally know enough to begin practicing philosophy.

For most of us, the combat is offensively ordinary. It is Friday afternoon and I need to stop working. There is a parent to call, money to give, a promise I would rather not keep. The task became mine while I was still comparing frameworks.

Sometimes duty includes defense and violence. Jews should not need this explained. This is also where the argument is most dangerous. Families can be abusive; a community can ask its members to do something evil. “This is my role” cannot settle whether I should do it. I have to judge what is being asked, with the knowledge that both obedience and refusal can do harm. Having a people gives that judgment consequences; it doesn't give me an exemption from making it.

Having children makes delay especially hard to defend. Identity is constructed, and parents spend every day constructing it. If I postpone transmitting a tradition while I solve comparative religion, my children will still grow up learning how to live. The surrounding culture will teach them what to celebrate, what to want, and what they owe other people. My hesitation helps determine their inheritance.

None of this proves Sinai. Ancestry and children explain why the question presses on me; revelation requires an argument I haven't made here. I have tried to explain why uncertainty no longer seems like a sufficient reason to keep Judaism at a distance. “Mine” matters to that question. It names my obligations to people and a way of life I already have reasons to care about. I can dispute what those obligations require without treating them as preferences I happen to have.

I once thought religion was what happened when people stopped searching. I have come to suspect my own insistence on remaining a searcher. Sometimes I was learning. Sometimes I was waiting for a commitment that would never expose me to being wrong, or ask more than I felt like giving.

I can see how a tradition was made and still accept its yoke. I will have to argue about what it asks of me, and I will sometimes have to change my mind. I have children to teach and a Shabbat to keep while I do.

Friday arrives whether or not the argument is finished, and I light the candles.
