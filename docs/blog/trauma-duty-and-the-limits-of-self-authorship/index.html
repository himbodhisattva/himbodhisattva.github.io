Trauma, Duty, and the Limits of Self-Authorship

Robert Kegan’s stages of adult development are easy to treat as a moral ranking. Stage 3 appears as a conventional person who follows the group; Stage 4, the GenX hero, an independent person who thinks for herself. In our self-focused culture, Stage 4 comes out looking enlightened and Stage 3 deficient.

This interpretation understates the strengths of Stage 3, and the demands of Stage 4. It also obscures an important feature of trauma. People can experience traumatic events at any developmental stage, but the modern concept/experience of trauma really only makes sense within a Stage 4 self: an injury that compromises a person’s self-governance.

The Difference Between Stage 3 and Stage 4

Kegan’s stages describe what a person can examine and what still operates as the perspective through which she experiences the world.

At Stage 2, the instrumental mind, the person has relatively stable wants and interests. Other people are understood in relation to those interests. Cooperation takes the form of a bargain: I will help you because you will help me, directly or because I value your approval.

At Stage 3, the socialized mind, relationships and shared standards become subject. Family, community, profession, or tradition answer the question “Who am I?” Statements such as “I am a father,” “I am an officer,” or “I am a Jew” do not refer to roles chosen by an independent self that exists prior to the identity; they name the identity directly.

At Stage 4, the self-authoring mind, the person can take these systems as object and can compare the expectations of her family and profession with those of her religion or political community and decide which principles she endorses. An internal system of judgment takes the place of governance by any one social system. This is a sort of high-modernism of the self condition, with attendant pros and cons.

This is an increase in psychological complexity, but it does not guarantee wisdom or moral worth. Someone at Stage 3 can be more honest/dependable than someone at Stage 4. Greater reflective capacity can support a serious moral system OR supply elaborate justifications for doing what one already wanted to do (implications for AI alignment left to the reader).

The Moral World of Stage 3

People raised in a culture of self-authorship (note: do not attempt to skip straight to stage 4 when raising your children.) have difficulty with the stage 3 perspective. They learn to ask what they want, which values feel authentic, and if an inherited expectation is something they want to continue choosing (as though it was chosen by the chain of ancestors before them). They may hear “I do it because it is my duty” as an admission that someone is living the unexamined life (I kinda get why they killed Socrates for that one now).

For a person at Stage 3, duty isn't an external force suppressing a "True Self". Duties and loyalties comprise the self. Only a shitty father first consults a private preference and then decides whether to act like a father. A police officer must not treat professional obligations as one lifestyle option among others. A member of a religious community who truly grew up in that system does not experience religious law as an alien demand that must justify itself before an inner Soviet Planning Committee.

This orientation has dangers. An institution can demand loyalty to corrupt norms, judge injuries it caused, or interpret dissent as betrayal. Yet Stage 3 also supports virtues that a culture of self-authorship tends to undervalue. It can sustain loyalty and a willingness to bear costs that bring no immediate personal benefit. It can preserve knowledge accumulated over generations instead of requiring each individual to build a moral world from first principles. That is, it is a substrate that supports long-running civilizations.

A tradition may contain arbitrary rules and inherited injustice. It may also contain hard-won knowledge about human rationalization and mechanisms for correcting error. A personal philosophy assembled recently from peers, therapy, school, or social media does not become more authoritative simply because it supports the fantasy of an inner True Self.

How Stage 2 Can Be Mistaken for Stage 4 - "Why therapy-speak is infuriating"

Something that gets pointed out in The Discourse that I have never quite seen explained in this form: why therapyspeak is so annoying. The rough pattern: selfish women and feminine men acting like they have transcended functional society ("the patriarchy"), talking down to the (more chud/masculine-coded) people who actually keep things running. Here's my model.

From the standpoint of Stage 3, Stage 4 can resemble a regression to Stage 2 - this is the dynamic that prevents that phase change; "I can't abandon my obligations to _". Both 2 and 4 devalue the social order. Either can reject a family expectation or communal norm, and both place the individual above the group without additional scaffolding.

The distinction lies in what governs that independence. Stage 2 pursues the person's wants. Stage 4 creates an internal authority capable of constraining them. A self-authored principle remains binding when it produces an unwelcome result.

Someone who invokes autonomy whenever a duty becomes burdensome and revises her principles whenever they frustrate her may not be displaying Stage 4 self-authorship. Therapeutic or political language can give Stage 2 instrumentalism a more sophisticated rationale while keeping the childlike selfishness. An allegedly self-authored person often remains highly socialized, though now by a diffuse peer culture rather than a stable institution. She still derives her moral vocabulary from other people; she has just changed which people count.

What appears to be independent judgment may be conformity to the current language of one’s social circle or political milieu. Someone at Stage 3 may lack the capacity to adjudicate fully among social systems, but can still be an excellent judge of whether a person keeps commitments when doing so becomes costly. That practical test can expose the distance between the language of self-authorship and its actual achievement.

Stage 4 is developmentally more complex than Stage 3. It does not follow that everyone who speaks its language has reached it or that every self-authored system is wiser than a traditional one. What we are usually seeing is not literal Stage 2. It is Stage 3 socialization into a moral culture that speaks in postmodern anti-system language and grants Stage 2 appetite unusual authority. The person remains group-authored; the group has taught her that being a good person means continually asserting that she is self-authored. This is what you get from the neglectful attempt to skip the socialized stage of development because Stage 3 is now enemy-coded to the progressive establishment.

Trauma and Self-Authorship

People at any stage can suffer horrible events and aftereffects like intrusive memories, conditioned fear, hypervigilance and dissociation. In a Stage 3 moral world, terrible events are interpreted through public and relational categories whereas trauma is a thing that happens to an isolated Self, a homo nullius loci.

Someone was attacked, failed in a duty, or acted immorally. A family was betrayed. A community may owe mourning or restitution. The injured person asks who was responsible, what duties remain, and how right relationship might be restored. This immediate contextualization means that the event can be better explained with words like grief, betrayal, injustice, or dishonor rather than trauma. The problem exists within the relation between the person and a moral order, rather than within the person.

Recovery therefore tends to have a public or relational form. The event is named and responsibility assigned. The person tells the story to people who share its meaning. Others remain present as the injured person returns, when possible, to a role that still matters. Pain may remain without determining which duties are binding. Fear is one reality to be weighed against other obligations.

The modern trauma framework asks a different question. It assumes that a person should govern herself through a coherent internal system, then considers what happens when her own reactions do not obey it. She knows that the danger is over, but her body continues to respond as though it were present. She decides that she wants intimacy or ordinary freedom, but an earlier experience continues to set the terms on which either is possible.

In this framework, trauma is a form of involuntary heteronomy. The person is governed by something she did not choose and does not endorse. Her stated beliefs no longer reliably direct her behavior.

Much of modern therapy addresses this conflict which is only present in the world created by the Stage 4 assumption of self-authorship. Therapy attempts to restore authorship over psychological processes that have escaped deliberate control.

Why Therapy Can Look Absurd from Stage 3

A duty-centered person expects therapy to restore capacities. Something happened and now he can't sleep, trust his judgment, remain present with his family, or do his job. Treatment should help him perceive reality accurately and return to right relationship with other people. Actual trauma treatment does not necessarily conflict with this expectation, though YMMV with therapists. The absurdity gets worse and widespread when clinical language migrates into moral language. "This makes me feel unsafe" changes from a report about my reaction into an accusation about your behavior.

This matters most in professions that exist because danger cannot always be avoided. A police officer, soldier, firefighter, ER nurse, etc. will judge psychological help by whether it restores sound judgment and the ability to protect or care for other people. A therapist focused on authenticity and emotional safety may be answering a different question from the one the client asked.

That client may happily accept help with sleep, panic, or decision-making under pressure while rejecting the idea that every distress signal should overrule an obligation. His rejection need not be denial; he may disagree with the therapist about what psychological health is for. The therapist may mean internal freedom/congruence; the client may mean becoming capable of fulfilling the roles that make his life meaningful.

Therapy can stop an old danger from making the current decision for him and so can widen the range of action available. It cannot supply the telos: once the person can choose again, therapy has not established what deserves to be chosen... and it is actually often terrible at assisting here, usually suggesting "more therapy."

It is not good that therapeutic vocabulary has become a public moral language. This is where the Stage 3 critique of contemporary therapeutic culture is right. Avoidance gets renamed boundary-setting, fear becomes proof of danger, external duties must justify themselves while internal appetite gets treated as More Fundamental and hence True.

What Each Stage Can See

Stage 4 is right that the social order is not identical with reality. Families and traditions can be wrong. An authority can forfeit its claim on a person, and recognizing that requires some capacity to stand outside the system.

Stage 3 is right that the self was not created ex nihilo. Relationships and institutions give us language and standards; they also transmit most of our knowledge and practical capacity. A person who imagines herself the sole author of her values is especially vulnerable to influences she cannot recognize. Therapeutic language is also inherited from a social world; it does not become the voice of an unmediated True Self just because its institutions are diffuse.

Our culture may be much worse at producing actual Stage 4 adults than its language suggests. Chapman argues that postmodern culture is shaped by the anti-system nihilism he calls Stage 4.5 even though most people lag behind its leading edge. A society that cannot sustain good Stage 3 formation will not reliably produce Stage 4 self-authorship merely by teaching its vocabulary.

Doing Stage 3 well is better than teaching Stage 3 people to despise the structures they still need. This does not mean any inherited moral order will do. A Stage 3 culture can socialize people into cowardice, cruelty, or nonsense. A good one provides obligations worth inheriting and enough internal honesty that its failures can eventually be taken as object. That is, it can produce self-authoring citizens, but only once they have inhabited a functional Socialized Self.
