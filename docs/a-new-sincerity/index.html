what is “a new sincerity”?

It's a band. The band is one guy, and he's me. Or rather: he's a character I wrote, the version of me who stayed a musician.

It's an attempt to find sincerity not despite self-awareness and detachment, but through them. It is about the discovery that knowing what you're doing is not the same as being able to do otherwise.

And it is full of tightly produced hyperpop bangers.

the album's argument

sorry for the self-awareness.

A New Sincerity is an attempt at infinite ironic detachment. Any point of view can be criticized, so the narrator tries to occupy all of them at once. He states the objection to every claim before anyone else can. When he confesses something, he also explains what the confession is doing. If he supplies the criticism himself, maybe nobody else gets to use it against him.

Usually, intelligent people use ironic detachment deftly enough that you don't notice it is an emotional defense mechanism. Stack it this high (or hand it to a dumbass like me The Protagonist) and the self-awareness becomes a giant sign pointing at whatever it was supposed to hide.

On (Unfortunately), the narrator's intelligence first makes his flaws charming. Then he uses it to win arguments and avoid responsibility. Soon he is trapped inside his own explanations. At the end, he mistakes one emotional breakthrough for a cure. He can explain exactly what he did wrong and still do it again. Then he can explain that too. Even spotting his vanity becomes something to be vain about.

The narrator is A New Sincerity. He is also me. When that becomes unflattering, I call him The Protagonist, who is definitely not me. This gives The Protagonist a convenient venue for demonstrating how exceptional he is, especially at renouncing the desire to be seen as exceptional.

The Protagonist would also like credit for recognizing this.

friday girls

wait, you like me too?!

Friday Girls sit outside the self-awareness thing. They're completely unselfaware love songs inspired by my marriage, framed so my daughters can self-insert. It just feels right. They are, literally, the best songs I've ever made.

And I love them so much that they give the whole project a new world to explore. I don't only have counterfactual me anymore; I have other characters to interact with. Fisher wrote about being haunted by futures that never arrived. Now I can animate the ghosts. My ghosts write songs. Maybe ghosts can't do anything, but songs and pictures can.

I can run this self-transformative process at multiple songs per day. It's fucked up.

the philosophy,
unfortunately.

  1. 01

    The New Sincerity: feel something despite knowing better.

    Wallace's anti-rebels refuse to let irony veto conviction. The point is not to lose self-awareness. It is to risk single-entendre values anyway: to know that the love song may be embarrassing, the apology inadequate, and the feeling compromised, then commit. Wallace proposed the move in 1993; Adam Kelly gave it the literary label “New Sincerity” in 2010.

    Wallace, “E Unibus Pluram” ↗Kelly, “David Foster Wallace and the New Sincerity” ↗

  2. 02

    Metamodernism: move between belief and disbelief.

    Metamodernism does not require sincerity to defeat irony. Hope and skepticism alternate without settling into a safe middle. I can know Friday Girls are invented and still love their songs. I can find the premise absurd while the chorus makes me cry. Neither reaction cancels the other.

    Vermeulen and van den Akker, “Notes on Metamodernism” ↗

  3. 03

    Performatism: build a situation in which belief can happen.

    This is the closest precedent for what I am doing. Instead of arguing myself into sincerity, I make the band, give the singers voices and histories, and write songs for them. Then I hear what those songs do to me. I know I built the frame, and I still respond to what happens inside it.

    Eshelman, “Performatism, or the End of Postmodernism” ↗

  4. 04

    Baudrillard's fatal strategies: intensify the simulation until it reverses.

    Do not try to recover some pure, unmediated reality. Push the system's own logic until it becomes more itself than itself and starts to reverse. The paradox does not provide a reliable program for emancipation. It is also the least reassuring strategy, which is part of its appeal.

    Baudrillard, Fatal Strategies ↗Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy ↗

All four strategies ask what a person should do in a world already saturated with representations. I can now manufacture new worlds of representation, populate them with fictional people, and collaborate with machines that make those people answer back.

A New Sincerity began as counterfactual autobiography. I made the version of me who never stopped writing emo and post-hardcore, then gave him my old recordings to remake. Friday Girls turned out to be something else. Through them I can write completely unselfconscious love songs inspired by my marriage. I frame the songs so my daughters can hear themselves in the singers. It feels right. They are also, literally, the best songs I have ever made.

Then A New Sincerity can encounter Friday Girls. Counterfactual me no longer has to haunt an empty timeline. He has other characters to admire and envy, then remix and misunderstand. Fisher wrote about being haunted by futures that never arrived. I can animate mine. My ghosts write songs. Maybe ghosts cannot do anything, but songs and pictures can.

I now run this process several times a day. That speed is exhilarating and deranging. Each pass gives another character a voice and a song I care about. My reaction changes what I make next before I have decided what the song means.

Producing real feeling is not the same as overcoming alienation. A manufactured world might enlarge my relationship with reality. It might become a higher-resolution substitute for it. I do not yet know which, and the answer may change from song to song. That unresolved problem is why “my therapist thinks I'm hilarious” belongs on the album.

The question is whether an experience becomes real because of where it came from, or because of what happens next.
